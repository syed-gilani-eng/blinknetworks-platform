## What "moving to the cloud" actually means

"Moving to the cloud" covers a range of changes, from replacing an on-premises email server with a hosted service, to running entire applications on infrastructure managed by a cloud provider. It does not have to mean an all-or-nothing shift—many organizations run a hybrid mix of cloud and on-premises systems for years.

## Common cloud service models in plain terms

- **Software as a Service (SaaS)** — using a ready-made application over the internet, such as email or productivity tools, without managing the underlying servers.
- **Infrastructure as a Service (IaaS)** — renting computing resources like virtual servers and storage from a cloud provider, while still managing the operating system and applications yourself.
- **Hybrid** — running some workloads on cloud infrastructure and others on-premises, connected together.

Most growing businesses already use some SaaS tools; the bigger decision is usually whether and when to move remaining on-premises infrastructure to IaaS or a fully managed cloud service.

## Signs it may be time to consider the cloud

- Aging on-premises hardware is approaching end of life and needs to be replaced regardless.
- Staff increasingly need secure access to systems and data from outside a single office location.
- The business is scaling, and on-premises capacity is becoming a bottleneck.
- Maintaining current infrastructure is consuming more internal time and attention than the business can reasonably spare.

## Signs it may not be the right time yet

- Specific applications have hard dependencies on local hardware or configurations that cannot yet be replicated in the cloud.
- The organization has not yet clarified its data security, compliance, or residency requirements well enough to choose a cloud configuration confidently.
- Recently purchased on-premises infrastructure still has significant useful life remaining, and there is no urgent driver to replace it early.

## A practical cloud-readiness checklist

- Current applications and data dependencies have been documented.
- Internet connectivity and bandwidth have been assessed for cloud-based workloads.
- Security and access requirements have been defined, not left for later.
- A rough budget comparison between continuing on-premises and moving to the cloud has been done.
- Staff who will be affected understand what will change for them.

## Common migration approaches

Two general approaches are common:

- **Lift-and-shift** — moving existing systems to cloud infrastructure with minimal changes, generally faster but not always taking full advantage of cloud-specific efficiencies.
- **Re-architecting** — redesigning applications and systems to take fuller advantage of cloud capabilities, generally more involved but potentially more efficient long-term.

Many organizations start with a lift-and-shift for lower-risk systems and consider re-architecting later, once they have more direct experience operating in the cloud.

## Cost and security considerations

Cloud costs are usually structured differently from on-premises costs—operating expenses rather than upfront capital purchases—which can be an advantage for cash flow but requires ongoing monitoring to avoid unexpected growth in usage-based costs. Security responsibilities are also shared between the cloud provider and the business, so it is important to understand which protections the provider manages and which remain the business's responsibility.

## Planning a phased move

Rather than moving everything at once, a phased approach—starting with lower-risk or higher-benefit systems—allows a business to build confidence and adjust its approach based on real experience before tackling more critical or complex workloads.
