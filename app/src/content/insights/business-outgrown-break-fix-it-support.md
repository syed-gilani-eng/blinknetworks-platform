Calling a technician when something breaks can be a sensible arrangement for a small business. You pay for the help you need, and there is no ongoing service commitment. But as more people depend on shared files, business applications and remote access, the cost of an interruption becomes harder to ignore.

The question is not whether your current technician is capable. It is whether the arrangement gives someone enough responsibility and time to prevent recurring problems and plan ahead.

For an owner or office manager in Toronto and the GTA, these five signs are useful reasons to review how IT support works. None automatically means you need an expensive contract. Each suggests a gap worth addressing.

## 1. The same problems keep returning

The shared drive disconnects again. A computer runs slowly every Monday. The office Wi-Fi improves after a restart, then becomes unreliable a few days later.

A quick repair may get people working, but repeated incidents deserve investigation. Ask whether anyone records the pattern, looks for the underlying cause and checks that the fix lasts. Otherwise, your business can keep paying to treat the same symptoms.

Imagine a small accounting office where several employees lose access to a shared application each week. Reconnecting them individually might clear the immediate queue. Investigating the network, application and server together could reveal a problem that affects the whole office. This is an illustrative scenario, not a BlinkNetworks client case.

At your next support review, bring three recurring issues and ask what would stop each one from returning.

## 2. An employee has become the unofficial IT department

Someone in the office knows how to reconnect the printer, reset passwords and call the software vendor. That person gradually becomes the first stop for almost every technology problem.

Their help has value, but it also has a cost. An operations manager interrupted several times a day is spending less time on operations. The arrangement also becomes fragile when that person is away or leaves the business.

For two weeks, record roughly how much time employees spend handling IT issues outside their normal responsibilities. Include troubleshooting, chasing suppliers and setting up new hires. Use the result to assess the support arrangement, not to criticise the person who has been holding it together.

## 3. You cannot get clear answers about protection and recovery

Ask who checks backups, who approves access and who responds to a security alert. If the answers depend on assumptions, you have an ownership problem.

The Canadian Centre for Cyber Security recommends assigning security responsibility, maintaining an incident response plan and addressing recovery as part of business continuity. These responsibilities still need an owner when technology support is outsourced. [Canadian baseline controls](https://www.cyber.gc.ca/en/guidance/baseline-cyber-security-controls-small-and-medium-organizations)

Request evidence in language you can use: the date of the last recovery test, outstanding security issues and the person responsible for each next action. You do not need a dashboard full of technical measurements to understand whether an important task was completed.

## 4. IT spending is difficult to plan

An emergency invoice is not necessarily evidence of poor service. Equipment fails, and businesses change. The warning sign is that almost every expense feels unexpected because nobody maintains a replacement plan or reviews upcoming needs.

Compare more than the technician's hourly rate. Consider repair charges, staff time lost, delayed customer work, subscriptions and equipment replacement. Avoid counting the same lost time twice when estimating the impact.

A managed service agreement can make some operating costs more predictable, but it does not make all IT spending fixed. Hardware, migrations, specialist work and after-hours support may be excluded. Ask for an example invoice and a written explanation of what generates additional charges before comparing proposals.

## 5. Business changes regularly turn into IT emergencies

A new employee arrives before their accounts are ready. An office move reveals that internet service was never ordered. A departing contractor still has access because nobody received the final instruction.

These situations point to missing processes between the business and its support provider. A repeatable onboarding checklist, an agreed offboarding process and a short planning meeting can help, regardless of how you purchase support.

Look ahead three months. Are you hiring, opening another location or replacing a major application? Decide who owns the technology work, what lead time they need and who approves the cost.

## Choose the level of support your business needs

Break-fix support can remain appropriate when interruptions have limited consequences and someone already owns maintenance and planning. Another business may benefit from scheduled maintenance alongside ad hoc support. [Managed IT](/insights/managed-it-services-guide/) may fit when ongoing oversight, user support and coordination need to sit with one provider.

Before signing an agreement, ask about support hours, response targets, exclusions, security responsibilities and the exit process. A response target means someone acknowledges or starts work on an issue; it is not necessarily a promise to resolve it within that time. Confirm that your business retains ownership and appropriate access to its accounts, documentation and data.

Start with one practical exercise: list your three most disruptive technology problems and the person accountable for improving each. If that list is difficult to complete, clarifying ownership is your first step.
